# 20.10 · Network Access Control (NAC) Penetration-Testing Method

> Framework component for adaptation. Publication does not authorize testing, certify conformance, or establish that an organization has adopted this practice.

## Admission to a network is not authorization to the hosts on it

Network Access Control decides whether a device may join a network, which identity or posture governs that decision, which segment or role it lands in, and what that position may then reach. Those are separate questions, and a NAC test answers them one at a time. Observing an 802.1X request does not establish that authentication can be bypassed; capturing an authentication exchange does not establish a recovered credential; matching an allowlisted MAC does not establish a trustworthy device; and landing on a segment does not establish authorization to test the hosts, directories, or applications reachable from it. The output is a defensible account of a *tested admission path* and its bounded consequence—not a claim that the network is open because a tool connected.

The central discipline of this method is that each primitive earns only the next question, never the final finding. A port that admitted a device presenting a copied identity has demonstrated admission under that identity at that port. Whether this is a control gap depends on the intended device policy and compensating controls; it has not, by itself, demonstrated lateral movement, directory compromise, or regulated-data exposure. Those are separate tests under separate authority.

Use this method with §20.1 (umbrella method), §10.1/10.2/10.4 (scope, rules, and engagement requirements), the adopter's evidence and reporting rules, and the appropriate safety controls for production. Its neighbors are intentionally distinct: §20.5 tests the network/protocol surface as a target, §20.6 tests hosts and directory infrastructure reached after admission, §20.9 tests wireless-tier access, and §20.11 covers physical entry to the jack. NAC is frequently the hinge between those methods—wireless and physical work hand *in* to it, and host/directory work hands *out* of it—but a handoff is a question routed to the owner, not an automatic extension of scope.

[NIST SP 800-115](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/115/final) informs the planning, execution, and analysis structure. [NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final) controls IA-3 (device identification and authentication), AC-3 (access enforcement), and AC-4 (information-flow enforcement, the segmentation control) are the natural mapping targets for NAC findings, applied against the adopter's actual control set. [IEEE 802.1X](https://1.ieee802.org/security/) and the EAP/RADIUS [RFCs](https://www.rfc-editor.org/) are protocol references, not a statement of what any specific deployment runs. [MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise](https://attack.mitre.org/) supplies a vocabulary for possible adversary behavior (hardware additions, adversary-in-the-middle, valid accounts, modify-authentication-process, network-boundary bridging, exploit-public-facing-application); a technique label describes a behavior, it does not prove one occurred, and the catalogue version should be taken from the living reference rather than a fixed ID set. Where a payment-card segmentation claim is in play, verify the currently applicable [PCI SSC standard and requirement](https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/document_library/) for that engagement; this document does not itself attest to a segmentation test or decide the applicable requirement. Where a deployment is being compared to a zero-trust model, note that network admission and resource-level access are different trust decisions: defeating admission does not equal reaching a resource behind a properly deployed per-session access overlay, and should be scoped and reported as such.

## Fix the path and the stopping point before the first frame

NAC testing nearly always requires a real connection position and often a position *behind* a legitimate device, so the authority and safety record has to be explicit before any active traffic.

| Decision | Record that makes the test bounded |
|---|---|
| Authority and position | Owner; approved connection points (which jacks, which sites, which bands if wireless-fronted); approved source device(s); test window; permitted test classes; and named contacts. A connection position is engagement authority, not a general license to test everything reachable from it. |
| Expected control | What the deployment is *supposed* to do at that port: whether 802.1X, device-identity fallback, posture assessment, dynamic segment/role assignment, or a pre-authentication landing segment applies, and who can confirm the intended behavior. "Admitted" is a hypothesis until the expected policy is known. |
| Safety and disruption ceiling | Rate/concurrency limits; production monitoring and deconfliction; a stop-and-escalation route; and separate, explicit authorization for each disruptive class—inline insertion behind a production endpoint, authentication-backend reachability induction (fail-open testing), traffic interception/relay, denial-of-service, and any administrative action on the control plane. Several of these can affect uninvolved users or whole segments from one action. |
| Data and evidence | Approved or synthetic test identities and device certificates; handling of any captured authentication material; capture scope; protected originals; redacted reporting copy; retention; and cleanup. Never upload captured credentials, certificates, configuration, or packet data to an unapproved service. |
| Coverage limit | What is unavailable or out of bounds: a jack or segment not reachable, a posture policy not disclosed, an identity not issued, an appliance not in scope, a device whose ownership is unknown, or a class the rules exclude. A limited vantage point limits the conclusion. |

The tester first verifies the actual starting state: what the port does to an unconfigured device, and what a known-good authorized device does by comparison. Capture the connection response, assigned segment, time, and expected result; coordinate monitoring as required. Start with the least disruptive observation adequate to classify the control, and escalate test classes only as the rules and the evidence warrant. Standing a rig up to a working state (host, test guest, adapters, inline-capable hardware) is addressed by §40.1/40.3/40.10; rig readiness is not engagement authority, and the ability of a rig to transmit a frame is not permission to transmit it.

## Classify the control before attacking it

For the approved position, observe and record what actually governs admission there: whether a supplicant exchange is requested; whether device-identity fallback is attempted; whether a device lands in a limited segment before or instead of authenticating; whether link-layer protection is present; what the assigned segment and its reachable services are; and which control plane or authentication backend is implied by the exchange. Reconcile each observation against the owner-supplied intended behavior. Mark **observed**, **documented but untested**, **in scope but unreachable**, **excluded**, and **ownership unknown** separately, and record when a vendor or configuration is *inferred* from a response rather than confirmed.

Use a compact row per admission decision, not one row per tool output:

| Field | Minimum useful record |
|---|---|
| Path | Source device and claimed identity → port/segment and the service or boundary at issue; time and environment. |
| Expected control | Who supplied the intended admission/identity/posture/segmentation rule and how current it is. |
| Contrast | A known-good authorized case, then one bounded negative or boundary case. |
| Observation | The actual exchange, assigned segment, posture response, or configuration evidence—not a tool label alone. |
| Decision | Confirmed effect, alternative explanation, untested extent, reviewer disposition, and any new authorization needed. |

## Select the test families that fit that control

Each family below is a question with an evidence bar and a ceiling, not a procedure to run because a tool exists. Select by the control actually present; record "not applicable" or "not tested" rather than silently omitting a family.

| Test family | Bounded question | Evidence and interpretation bar · ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Supplicant authentication (802.1X / EAP) and client trust | Does the port require the intended authentication, and does a representative client validate the authentication server's identity before presenting a secret? | An observed request for authentication, or a client that omits server validation, is a configuration state—not a recovered credential or network access. Capturing a challenge/response is not a cracked secret; record the actual recovery result and key-handling boundary. Credential capture via a rogue authentication server affects the specific client and needs explicit rules. |
| Device-identity fallback (MAC-based admission) | Where authentication is unavailable and the deployment falls back to a device allowlist, does that fallback admit a device presenting a copied or class-matching identity? | A MAC address alone is not a strong device authenticator. Check whether fallback, profiling, inventory and monitoring make the observed admission expected or excessive. A copied identity admitted to a segment proves that local decision under those conditions—not a broader control failure or trustworthiness of anything on that segment. |
| Posture / compliance assessment | Does the deployment actually enforce the endpoint-health checks it claims (and does an absent or stale checker fail open to a higher-trust segment)? | Change one posture attribute at a time from a known-good baseline and observe the response; distinguish "the attribute is checked" from "the attribute is assumed." A reporting agent that can be made to assert compliance locally defeats posture integrity for that device; it does not by itself establish broad access. Agent tampering on a tester-controlled endpoint is in scope only under the rules. |
| Dynamic segment / role assignment | Is the segment or role the deployment assigns on admission the one intended for that identity/posture, and can an admitted position influence that assignment? | The assigned segment and its reachable services are the evidence; a richer-than-expected assignment is a finding when corroborated against the intended policy. An assignment attribute observed in an exchange is not proof it can be forged. |
| Pre-authentication, guest, restricted, and critical (fail-open) landing segments | What can each limited-services landing segment actually reach, and does the backend becoming unreachable cause the port to grant access rather than deny it? | Map actual reach against the owner-confirmed intended reach; access beyond that boundary may be a finding when the path and effective controls are established. Inducing backend unreachability to test fail-open is production-disruptive and requires explicit, separate authorization; confirm the intended fail-open vs. fail-closed posture at scoping and prefer confirming configuration over inducing the condition. |
| Segment-crossing / VLAN-boundary tests | From the admitted position, can the device cross a boundary the owner expected to deny (including to a regulated or management segment)? | State the expected rule, both the source and the destination, the observed service behavior, and a control path. A reachable service without the policy is not a segmentation failure, and a regulated-segment conclusion needs the owner-confirmed boundary. Boundary-crossing techniques that depend on trunk/native-segment misconfiguration are tested within the disruption ceiling. |
| Authentication-backend / RADIUS plane | Is the enforcement-point-to-backend channel integrity-protected and its shared trust strong, or does it carry admission decisions over an unprotected transport without message authentication? | Record the protocol, authentication flow, message-authenticator enforcement, transport and compensating controls. An applicable unprotected response path can support a reportable forgery precondition without a live forgery demo; it is not automatically a demonstrated bypass. A forgery demonstration requires an authorized on-path position and explicit rules; verify any named protocol-forgery class against the primary advisory and the deployed build rather than assuming applicability. |
| Captive / onboarding portal | Can the approved client reach destinations contrary to the stated policy before or after portal acceptance, including by presenting a copied identity of an already-authorized client? | Portal appearance is not proof of network encryption, and acceptance is not proof of authorized reach. A copied identity is one possible bypass hypothesis, not a presumed successful path; record association, the actual data boundary, and any disclosed test-only data separately. Route portal web-application depth to §20.2/20.3. |
| Inline / transparent-bridge bypass | Where authentication is not protected at the host edge and the port does not force re-authentication on a link-state change, can a device inserted between an authenticated endpoint and the port ride the authenticated session? | A demonstrated inline bypass may be consequential, but its impact depends on the exact segment and reachable services. Insertion behind a production endpoint requires explicit authorization. Where link-layer protection is present, confirm whether it is enforced at the tested host edge or only on uplinks; coexistence is not a demonstrated bypass. Evidence is the forwarded authentication plus tester traffic egressing under the authenticated identity, with reach proof. |
| Management / control plane and appliance currency | Is the NAC control plane (administrative interfaces and APIs) protected as expected, and is the deployed appliance current against its vendor's advisories? | An exposed login, suspected default credential or absent second factor is a lead or control-state observation, not control-plane compromise. Establish the effective policy and authorized access result before naming a finding. Confirm appliance version against vendor advisories and applicable known-exploited-vulnerability entries *at engagement time*; a version match alone is not proof of exploitation. Administrative action, policy change and vulnerability exploitation require named-target authorization and a rollback path. |

Modern deployments add branches the older admission checklist will not settle by itself: link-layer encryption bootstrapped by varying authentication strength; onboarding and device-profiling systems that classify rather than authenticate; cloud-managed or distributed enforcement; and the NAC-versus-resource-access trust boundary discussed above. Select a branch only when the approved deployment exposes it, then route to the specialist method or an explicit scope extension. Clinical, operational-technology, or other safety-sensitive segments require their own authorization and safety design; proximity on a network diagram is not permission.

## From primitive to demonstrated impact

These admission-bypass chain families—authentication bypass to network access, device-identity impersonation, posture bypass, segment crossing, interception, portal bypass, control-plane compromise, segmentation gap, backend compromise, inline bridge, protocol forgery, and appliance compromise—are hypotheses that help choose the next check, not prewritten findings. Language that would call every reachable system "brought into scope" or assign a fixed Critical/High from a single primitive is not used here. Use four evidence states:

1. **Observed** — an approved device, at an approved port, produced an actual response: a request for authentication was seen, a segment was assigned, a posture check reacted, or a safe test action returned a result. Preserve the exact exchange, position, time, and limits.
2. **Corroborated** — the observation is confirmed against the owner's intended policy and a second signal (inventory, configuration, or a known-good contrast), so the control state is established rather than inferred from one response.
3. **Demonstrated under authority** — a bounded, authorized action produced the prohibited outcome: an admitted position reached a named, owner-approved fixture across a boundary expected to deny it, or an inline position carried tester traffic under an authenticated identity. The claim is the exact path exercised—not every sibling port, segment, or host.
4. **Not tested** — the next edge would require a credential not recovered, a host or directory on the admitted segment, another method, a disruptive action, data access, or a configuration change outside the rules. Route it to the owner as a separately scoped question rather than writing the imagined final step as fact.

**Synthetic admission example.** From an approved test position, the tester observes that a port falls back to device-identity admission when no authentication is offered, and that a device presenting a copied printer-class identity is admitted to a segment. A known-good authorized device and a bounded negative case rule out a mistaken position. The report can say that, at that port, under those conditions, a device presenting that class identity was admitted to that segment, and can enumerate what that segment was observed to reach under the rules. It cannot say the network is compromised, that every port behaves this way, that the admitted position implies access to any host on the segment, or that a regulated environment was reached—unless that exact boundary was in scope, the expected policy was confirmed, and the crossing was separately demonstrated under authority. If the intended fallback behavior was never confirmed with the owner, this is a policy question or a coverage note before it is a finding.

Technical reach can suggest impact or a next question; only written authority changes assessment scope. Severity follows demonstrated effect, exploit preconditions, exposure, business context, and the adopter's scoring model—not the test family, a protocol name, or a tool label. An ATT&CK mapping describes behavior, not a rating.

## Report, restore, and hand off

A useful finding packet contains the expected admission/segmentation control, the exact port/position and device/identity used, the authorized procedure and its known-good contrast, authentic protected evidence, a redacted recipient copy, the observed effect, competing explanations, the bounded consequence, remediation direction (which often implies a network change—re-authentication on link change, link-layer protection, backend transport protection or message authentication, trunk/native-segment hardening, posture enforcement, allowlist hygiene, or appliance patching—so note the change-management impact), reviewer disposition, and a retest condition. Where the work touches a payment-card or other segmentation obligation, map only the applicable requirement after the scope and evidence are checked, and distinguish an owner-confirmed exception from a control failure.

NAC is a hinge, so the handoffs matter and each is a fresh authorization check, not an automatic grant. Wireless-tier access (§20.9) and physical entry to the jack (§20.11) hand *in* to this method; once a device is admitted, the wire/protocol-as-target surface routes to §20.5, host and directory exploitation on the reached systems routes to §20.6, and database and web/API behavior route to §20.7 and §20.2/20.3. Preserve the authorized starting position and the tested endpoint across each handoff; a plausible cross-method chain is only as strong as its individually demonstrated links.

At close, reconcile every temporary artifact: issued and captured identities and certificates (rotation/revocation with the owner), any device-identity allowlist entries added for the test, any inline device inserted, any spoofed identity reset, and any administrative or policy change made under authority. Verify owner-confirmed restoration rather than assuming cleanup because the test window ended.

Automation can help with bounded admission observation, segment-reach mapping, appliance-version-to-advisory correlation, and candidate sorting. It must inherit the same connection and target allowlist, disruption ceiling, identity and credential custody, stop route, provenance, and human finding disposition. Pilot orchestration-skill concepts and broad attack-class additions are design material for a future lane—not evidence of a deployed orchestrator or a validated test library. Human authority remains especially important for inline insertion, backend-reachability induction, interception/relay, control-plane action, confirmed-vulnerability exploitation, and any crossing into a regulated or safety-sensitive segment.

The output of this method is a reviewable account of which devices and identities could gain admission at a defined position, under which trust decision, to which segment, and what that position could and could not then reach. It is neither a certification nor a claim that a specific network passed every test family above.
